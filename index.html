A twin-hull autonomous catamaran that surveys lakes and rivers on its own — planning a route from a cloud dashboard, dodging obstacles with stereo-camera vision, and lowering an instrumented pod to sample water quality at depth. A Raspberry Pi 5 does the thinking; two microcontrollers handle the real-time work; and the entire stack runs in simulation before it ever touches water.

Result v1 completed autonomous waypoint surveys on local lakes. v2 rebuilds it around a Pi 5 brain, stereo-vision obstacle avoidance, and a 4G cloud command center — validated end-to-end in simulation and now moving onto the water.
Waypoint planner v1 firmware
Watch
Version 1 · Field-tested

It started as a sketch: a stable, twin-hull platform that could carry sensitive instruments across open water and still sit calm enough to take clean readings.

The dual-hull layout keeps the boat steady; each pontoon houses waterproof compartments for batteries and electronics. The winch lowers an instrumented pod on a tethered slip ring until it registers bottom contact, then samples water temperature, conductivity (salinity), and light before retracting and moving to the next waypoint.

CAD · v1 platform

v1 runs on three Raspberry Pi Picos in MicroPython: a boat controller juggling nine concurrent async tasks — GPS navigation, heading, dual-brushless drive, winch, mission logic, and safety — plus a shore operator station and the underwater pod.

They talk over an HC-12 433 MHz half-duplex radio with XOR-checksummed frames and burst/listen timing. The shore station loads a JSON mission, shows live telemetry, logs everything to CSV, and can e-stop or take manual control at any moment.

STEP 01 — HULL

A hull tuned for the water

The twin-hull geometry is refined in CAD for stability and clean flow, then printed in bolt-together sections. v1 printed in PLA; v2 moves to tougher ASA with a fiberglass-and-epoxy skin for stiffness and water resistance.

STEP 02 — BOARD

One board instead of a harness

A custom KiCad carrier board replaces v1's hand-wired loom — consolidating three isolated battery domains, the two drive ESCs, and every sensor header in one place. Fewer failure points, faster to service.

STEP 03 — BRAIN

A Pi 5 that can see

A Raspberry Pi 5 runs navigation, the mission state machine, and a stereo-camera vision pipeline — YOLO detection gated by a cheap water-anomaly screen — while the two RP2040s stay down at the real-time layer, driving motors and reading sensors.

Custom PCB · rev 01 → rev 02

Two USB cameras form a stereo pair; StereoSGBM turns the disparity into a real per-object distance, so a detected obstacle isn't just a box on a screen — it has a range.

A three-tier vision pipeline keeps it cheap: a constant water-anomaly screen wakes a YOLOv8-nano detector only when something's there, and a threat score decides what to track and when to steer away.

The whole stack is simulation-first: navigation, the mission state machine, the vision pipeline, and the cloud uploader all run on a laptop simulator before any hardware is attached. The mission itself is a state machine — transit → deploy → sample → retract → advance — with geo-fenced boundaries and automatic return-to-home on low battery.

The platform is modular by design — the pod bay and winch take payloads beyond water quality: a sonar module for bathymetric mapping, cameras for underwater surveys, sediment or water-collection tools, aquatic-vegetation or debris monitoring. The shore station's JSON missions and live telemetry don't care what's attached, so it works as a general-purpose autonomous surface vessel anyone could adapt by swapping the pod.

It's ongoing — finishing hardware bring-up on the custom PCB, tuning the vision-guided avoidance on the water, and, eventually, coordinating multiple boats across a larger survey area.

Design & manufacturing

Every robot was designed from scratch in CAD, with weapon systems refined through iterative simulation and real-world testing — horizontal spinners and vertical drums.

Manufacturing paired outsourced CNC-machined aluminum frames with in-house 3D-printed armor in reinforced nylon and polycarbonate.

The robots
Top 35 US · 2025 · 3 lb beater

Korybantes — a 3 lb beater built for reliability and fast repair between matches.

6061 aluminum, carbon-fiber plate, laser-cut titanium armor, UHMW impact structures, and in-house 3D-printed assemblies for rapid iteration — the CNC machining and heat-treating were sent out to a shop. The beater delivers consistent kinetic output that holds up under repeated impacts.

Every so often I'd get a design check from senior builders in the community and fold their criticism into the structural reinforcement and drivetrain tuning. Korybantes reached a top-35 U.S. ranking at the 2025 National 3 lb competition.

Korybantes CAD
Top 15 US · 2025 · 1 lb metal antweight

Achilles (metal) — a 1 lb horizontal spinner, the metal evolution of my plastic antweight.

Flexible, modular 3D-printed armor in engineering-grade polymers absorbs impact and swaps fast between matches, while the weapon and structure use AR500 steel and Grade-5 titanium for durability and energy transfer. Every part is designed around real processes — laser cutting, precision machining, and additive-manufacturing tolerances.

Achilles reached a top-15 U.S. ranking and competed at the 2025 National Antweight competition.

Achilles CAD
Shuffler drive · custom cam geometry

Instead of wheels, the newest version of Achilles walks on a shuffler drive I designed around a single custom cam.

The cam profile drives feet on both the top and the bottom of the chassis, so the robot keeps shuffling forward even after it's flipped — no self-righting mechanism needed, and no dead side to get stuck on mid-match.

4th National · Top 30 US · 2024 · 1 lb plastic AW

Achilles (plastic) — the 1 lb belt-driven undercutter that started it all.

Designed and printed in CAD with a modular plastic chassis for fast, reliable repairs. I handled the CAD, soldering, and wiring, tuning the weight distribution to manage the spinner's gyroscopic forces.

It placed 4th at a national plastic-antweight competition and ranked among the top 30 in the U.S. (2024) — and helped shape the low-profile, belt-driven undercutter meta in the Northern California scene.

More of the roster

After every match, win or lose, we'd pull the robot apart and work out what broke and why — every design generation came straight out of that damage analysis, so I learned to design for repairability first.

The best part was teaching. As club president I ran workshops on CAD, soldering, physics, and fabrication — and a few members went on to build and compete with robots of their own.

A tank-drive maze-navigation robot built with a partner for Northeastern's Cornerstone of Engineering course — I led the mechanical CAD and the MicroPython firmware. An asyncio control loop juggles the sensors, drive, an LCD, and real-time pathfinding on a single Raspberry Pi Pico.

Result Maps and solves an unknown maze autonomously — concurrent sensing, motor control, and navigation on one microcontroller.
Onshape CAD Firmware
Watch

The chassis is a compact, low-slung tank-drive platform designed in Onshape, with custom mounts I modeled for every board and sensor so nothing rattles loose on the move.

Ultrasonic sensors handle distance and obstacle detection while IR sensors follow the line and read proximity; an I2C LCD and status LEDs give live feedback, and a bench potentiometer tunes behavior without a reflash.

An asyncio-based control loop runs sensor polling, motor control, and navigation as concurrent tasks on one Raspberry Pi Pico — responsive obstacle avoidance without ever blocking the main loop. The robot builds an internal map of visited cells and backtracks to explore unvisited branches.

That same asyncio architecture carried straight into the survey boat's firmware — concurrent tasks on one Pico, just on water instead of in a maze.
