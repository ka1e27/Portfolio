A twin-hull autonomous catamaran that surveys lakes and rivers on its own — planning a route from a cloud dashboard, dodging obstacles with stereo-camera vision, and lowering an instrumented pod to sample water quality at depth. A Raspberry Pi 5 does the thinking; two microcontrollers handle the real-time work; and the entire stack runs in simulation before it ever touches water.

Result v1 completed autonomous waypoint surveys on local lakes. v2 rebuilds it around a Pi 5 brain, stereo-vision obstacle avoidance, and a 4G cloud command center — validated end-to-end in simulation and now moving onto the water.
Watch
Version 1 · Field-tested

It started as a sketch: a stable, twin-hull platform that could carry sensitive instruments across open water and still sit calm enough to take clean readings.

The dual-hull layout keeps the boat steady; each pontoon houses waterproof compartments for batteries and electronics. Sensor pods measure temperature, salinity, light penetration, and depth, and connect to the main bus through waterproof magnetic connectors — swap a pod in the field with no tools.

CAD · v1 platform

The control system runs on two Raspberry Pi Picos — one dedicated to navigation and motor control, the other to sensor acquisition and logging.

They share a UART bus, while an HC-12 433 MHz radio streams live telemetry back to a shore station with over a kilometre of range across open water.

STEP 01 — HULL

A hull designed, not improvised

Where v1 was built by hand, v2 starts in CAD. The new hull tightens the internal layout around the electronics and prints in sections that bolt together.

STEP 02 — BOARD

One board instead of a harness

A custom KiCad carrier board replaces v1's hand-wired loom — consolidating three isolated battery domains, the two drive ESCs, and every sensor header in one place. Fewer failure points, faster to service.

STEP 03 — BRAIN

A Pi 5 that can see

A Raspberry Pi 5 runs navigation, the mission state machine, and a stereo-camera vision pipeline — YOLO detection gated by a cheap water-anomaly screen — while the two RP2040s stay down at the real-time layer, driving motors and reading sensors.

The whole stack is simulation-first: navigation, the mission state machine, the vision pipeline, and the cloud uploader all run on a laptop simulator before any hardware is attached. The mission itself is a state machine — transit → deploy → sample → retract → advance — with geo-fenced boundaries and automatic return-to-home on low battery.

It's ongoing — finishing hardware bring-up on the custom PCB, tuning the vision-guided avoidance on the water, and, eventually, coordinating multiple boats across a larger survey area.

Design & manufacturing

Every robot was designed from scratch in CAD, with weapon systems refined through iterative simulation and real-world testing — horizontal spinners, vertical drums, and overhead hammers.

Manufacturing paired CNC-machined aluminum frames with 3D-printed armor in reinforced nylon and polycarbonate. Critical joints used press-fit bushings and custom fastener patterns to survive impacts.

Results

The team competed at the 2024 and 2025 Robot Combat League National Championships, with a best finish of 4th nationally in Plastic Antweight.

After every match, win or lose, we'd pull the robot apart and work out what broke and why. Each design generation came straight out of that damage analysis — so I learned to design for repairability first. A robot that can't be fixed between matches doesn't last a tournament.

The roster

The best part was teaching. As club president I ran workshops on CAD, soldering, physics, and fabrication — and a few members went on to build and compete with robots of their own.

An autonomous maze-navigation robot built for Northeastern's Cornerstone of Engineering course. I handled the mechanical CAD and the entire MicroPython firmware stack — sensor fusion, motor control, and real-time pathfinding, all on a single Pico.

Result Maps and solves an unknown maze autonomously — concurrent sensing, motor control, and navigation on one microcontroller.
Watch

The chassis was designed in Onshape around two goals: keep it compact, and keep the center of gravity low. A differential drive with micro gear motors and optical encoders gives precise odometry.

Five forward IR sensors provide a 180° view for obstacle detection; two side-mounted sensors handle wall tracking. Their readings are fused with weighted averaging to reject noise.

Tuning
PID + nav params over serial — retune without reflashing

An asyncio-based control loop runs sensor polling, motor control, and navigation as concurrent tasks on one Raspberry Pi Pico — responsive obstacle avoidance without ever blocking the main loop. The robot builds an internal map of visited cells and backtracks to explore unvisited branches.

That same asyncio architecture carried straight into the survey boat's firmware — concurrent tasks on one Pico, just on water instead of in a maze.
